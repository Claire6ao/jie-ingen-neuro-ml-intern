# Week 1 Recap — InGen Physical AI and the Neuro-ML Bridge

## Most Analogous InGen Platform: Sentinel Prime AI

Among the InGen platforms examined this week, **Sentinel Prime AI** is the most methodologically analogous to an EEG motor-imagery decoding system.

The similarity is not based on the physical sensors themselves. EEG electrodes measure electrical activity generated by the brain, whereas Sentinel uses physical sensors such as RGB, thermal, acoustic, radar, and LiDAR-based sensing. Instead, the strongest connection is the computational structure of the two systems.

An EEG motor-imagery pipeline can be summarized as:

$$
X_{\text{EEG}}
\rightarrow
\text{preprocessing}
\rightarrow
\text{feature extraction}
\rightarrow
\text{classification}
\rightarrow
\hat{y}.
$$

For example, Common Spatial Patterns (CSP) can transform multichannel EEG into discriminative components, after which a classifier such as Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) predicts the motor-imagery class.

Sentinel follows an analogous structure:

$$
X_{\text{sensor}}
\rightarrow
\text{modality-specific processing}
\rightarrow
\text{sensor fusion}
\rightarrow
\text{classification}
\rightarrow
\text{decision}.
$$

In both cases, the system begins with noisy, high-dimensional observations and attempts to recover a task-relevant state. The mathematical bridge is therefore **multichannel signal decoding**, rather than a superficial comparison between EEG electrodes and robot sensors.

Sentinel also extends the classical neural-decoding pipeline by explicitly considering predictive uncertainty before converting a classification into an operational decision. This is especially important in physical AI because an incorrect prediction can produce consequences in the physical environment.

## PIC 2.0 Model Closest to a Neural Decoder: SEOM

Among the PIC 2.0 foundation-model concepts, **SEOM** is the closest conceptual analogue to a neural decoder because it can be understood in terms of constructing a useful internal representation of observed state.

A neural decoder performs a transformation such as:

$$
X_{\text{neural}}
\rightarrow
z
\rightarrow
\hat{y}_{\text{behavior}},
$$

where $X_{\text{neural}}$ contains measured neural signals, $z$ represents extracted or learned features, and $\hat{y}$ is an estimate of behavioral state.

The corresponding physical-AI structure is:

$$
X_{\text{sensor}}
\rightarrow
z
\rightarrow
\hat{s}_{\text{environment}}.
$$

Although the physical meaning of the inputs differs, both systems attempt to transform high-dimensional observations into a representation that preserves information relevant to an underlying state.

## Key Takeaway

The main lesson from Week 1 is that the strongest connection between neuroscience and physical AI is **mathematical rather than sensor-specific**. Methods developed for neural decoding — including multichannel representation, spatial filtering, spectral analysis, dimensionality reduction, classification, temporal state estimation, and uncertainty analysis — provide a useful framework for understanding robot-sensor problems.

The recurring computational structure is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{observations}
\rightarrow
\text{representation}
\rightarrow
\text{state estimation}
\rightarrow
\text{decision}
}
$$

This neuro-ML perspective provides the methodological foundation for applying signal-processing and machine-learning techniques to InGen's physical-AI problems in the following weeks.
