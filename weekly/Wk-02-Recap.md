# Week 02 Recap — Robot Sensor PSD vs. EEG Frequency-Band Analysis

This week focused on transferring frequency-domain analysis methods that I had previously encountered in EEG analysis to synthetic Aido Rover sensor data. The main similarity between the two domains is that the raw time-series signal alone does not fully describe the underlying state. In EEG, power spectral density (PSD) is used to decompose neural activity into frequency bands such as Delta, Theta, and Alpha, allowing differences in cognitive state to be examined in the frequency domain. The same idea can be applied to robot telemetry: motor-current and IMU signals can be transformed using Welch PSD, and their energy can be summarized within low-, medium-, and high-frequency bands.

For the robot data, the frequency structure was strongly related to operational mode. PATROL was primarily associated with lower-frequency activity, while ALERT introduced stronger medium-frequency components. FAULT showed the clearest high-frequency signature. In the window-level analysis, motor-current high-band energy was almost perfectly associated with FAULT (r = 0.999), while IMU high-band energy showed a similarly strong relationship (r = 0.998). IMU low-frequency energy was strongly associated with PATROL (r = 0.982), while medium-frequency energy was strongly associated with ALERT (r = 0.983). These results demonstrate that the same basic feature-extraction idea used for EEG frequency bands can also create useful robot-state features.

The largest difference is the interpretation of the frequencies themselves. EEG frequency bands have physiological meaning derived from neural oscillations and established neuroscience literature. In contrast, the low (0–0.5 Hz), medium (0.5–2 Hz), and high (2–5 Hz) robot bands in this exercise represent mechanical and operational dynamics rather than biological rhythms. A high-frequency component in a robot signal may indicate vibration, rapid motor-current fluctuation, or mechanical instability rather than a cognitive or neural process.

Another difference was the strength of the observed relationships. In the EEG confusion project, frequency-band relationships with confusion were relatively weak and noisy, which is expected for biological data affected by individual variability and many uncontrolled factors. The synthetic robot data produced much clearer separation between states. This was stronger than I initially expected, but it is also a consequence of the simulation design: mode-specific frequency components were intentionally embedded into the synthetic signals.

Overall, the most transferable concept is not the specific frequency ranges, but the pipeline: time-series signal → PSD → band energy → state-related features. The meaning of those features changes substantially between neuroscience and robotics, but the underlying signal-processing framework remains highly reusable.
